Timed pill dispenser

A portable dispenser that attaches to a water bottle, holds four medications, and releases them at up to 16 programmable times a day.

Course
MECH 307, final report May 2026
Team
Team project, three students
Controller
Arduino Nano ESP32, DS3231 real-time clock
Hardware
OLED, 2 MG90S servos, 4 IR break-beam pairs, buzzer, 3 buttons, potentiometer
Made with
SolidWorks, 3D printing, C++ on Arduino

Problem

Help someone take up to four medications on schedule, in a device small enough to carry on a water bottle.

Approach

Servo-driven plungers, IR fill sensing, clock-based scheduling, and an OLED menu with a display priority system.

Result

All six subsystems met their requirements, and the rare jams cleared with a slight shake.

Takeaway

Sliding printed parts need early, repeated test prints, and shared CAD reviews keep teammates' parts from colliding.

How it works

The user sets dispense times for each of the four pill slots with two buttons and a menu on the OLED. When a scheduled time arrives, a buzzer chimes and the screen alerts the user. Pressing the dispense button runs one or both servos, which turn a gear that drives a plunger to push a pill out of its vertical slot into a pull-out tray on the left or right side. An LED shows which tray received the pills.

Four IR break-beam sensor pairs watch the fill level of each slot. When a slot drops to roughly three pills, the beam is no longer blocked and the screen shows a refill message for that slot. Each slot has a sliding door for reloading and a spring stopper at the top to keep the sensor readings consistent. A potentiometer sets how often the chime beeps and can turn it off.

The software

The main loop reads the clock, polls the sensors and compares the time with every saved schedule. A flag stops the same schedule from firing twice in one minute. Because several functions want the screen at once, the code uses a priority order: dispensing first, then menus, then refill warnings, then the clock. That kept messages from overwriting one another.

Results

The finished device met the requirements for all six subsystems. The dispensing mechanism worked consistently, with very few jams, and those cleared with a slight shake of the device.

What was hard

Sliding parts were the hardest to tolerance. Getting the pill doors to move freely without letting a pill slip out took many print iterations, each costing hours, and it depended on print orientation and supports as much as on clearances. Parts designed by different teammates also collided in the assembly until we started meeting regularly and sharing models. On the electronics side, the OLED and clock both use I2C, which we hadn't covered in class, so we had to learn the wiring, libraries and setup on our own.

More mechatronics work

Bat Bot: StrongRAM 2025 competition robot

MECH 202 · Team project, four students

A Batman Tumbler-styled robot built to push a truck, lift a lacrosse ball onto a pedestal, flip flagpoles and hang from a chin-up bar. It has two driven wheels and two casters, a 3D-printed chassis redesigned three times, a chain-and-scoop lift that handles both the ball and the flagpoles, and a two-servo chin-up arm. We started with a belt-driven lift, found the belts either too stiff or too stretchy, and switched to bike chain. We stayed under both our electronics and non-electronics budgets. The chin-up arm never held the robot fully off the ground before the deadline, and the report says so.
