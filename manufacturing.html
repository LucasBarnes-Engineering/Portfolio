What the package contains

A bill of materials with part numbers, material, supplier and lead time for every item, from the 1215 steel blank to the carbide face mill, #3 center drill, 32 DP gear cutter and 6-32 set screw. Then four process sheets, each written so someone else could set up and run the operation without asking questions.

Why it matters

The sheets build in the checks a machinist would otherwise learn the hard way: take the chuck key out, measure with calipers because the DRO isn't always accurate, and verify indexing with a trial notch before committing to full depth. It's the same documentation habit I want to bring to production work.

Lab projects

Screwdrivers: forged against molded

Introduction to manufacturing

Made one screwdriver by drop forging, heat treating and machining a tool steel blade with a knurled handle, and one with an injection-molded handle. Costing both showed why process choice matters: about $231 and 11 hours of labor for the forged one, against about $7 and 15 minutes for the molded one. My proposal for the forged version was to fold the knurling into the CNC lathe and automate the forging.

Clock: manual mill and lathe

Introduction to manufacturing

A desk clock with pen and pencil holders, machined from brass, steel and aluminum on manual machines in about 30 hours, at roughly $646 in materials and labor. Reworking the plan around a CNC lathe and mill and a laser-cut face brought my estimate down to about 3 hours and $106 per clock, around six times cheaper.

The lesson I kept: rushing a manual operation always cost me more time than slowing down.

Writing on manufacturing

When should an R&D team 3D print, and when should it machine?

JTC 300, Strategic Writing and Communication · 20-page position and recommendation report · May 2026

I reviewed peer-reviewed and government research on additive manufacturing (AM) in R&D and argued for a middle path: use AM as the main tool for early prototyping, where speed, design freedom and low tooling cost win, and plan the handoff to traditional methods for validation, load-bearing and fatigue-critical parts, and higher quantities. Rather than picking a side, the report recommends a criteria-based decision framework and a repeatable workflow.

  • Where AM wins: no fixtures or toolpaths, so a design can change in the morning and be tested the next day, and the cost per part stays nearly flat at small quantities.
  • Where it loses: cost per part overtakes machining as quantities grow, printed metal can behave differently along and across the build direction and often needs post-processing, and machining still holds tighter tolerances and finishes.
  • Stated limits: the report uses secondary sources only and no cost data from a real organization, so the crossover points will differ from case to case.

The six-phase workflow
  1. Scope. Classify the prototype as a concept model, form-and-fit check, functional prototype or pre-production article, and state the mechanical, thermal and dimensional requirements up front.
  2. Screen. Score the prototype against the criteria above and route it to printing or to machining or an outside vendor.
  3. Pilot. Run the first build conservatively and record orientation, scan strategy and any heat treatment so the result can be reproduced.
  4. Validate. Inspect dimensions and, where structure matters, test witness coupons printed alongside the part.
  5. Decide. Document whether the printed part works as is, needs post-processing, or should be re-sourced for the next iteration.
  6. Review. Revisit the decision each quarter as materials, printers and qualification data change.
